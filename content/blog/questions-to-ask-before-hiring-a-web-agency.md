Hiring a web or Shopify agency is one of the bigger bets a small business makes. A good one gives you a site that sells and that you can run yourself. A bad one leaves you with a store you do not own, a bill that keeps growing, and nobody to call when checkout breaks on a Friday night.

Most of the problems we see are not about design or code. They are about things nobody asked before the contract was signed. These are the 10 questions we would ask if we were hiring an agency ourselves, with the answers you want to hear and the ones that should make you pause.

## 1. Who will own the website, the domain and the accounts?

This is the most important question on the list.

**A good answer:** "You do. The domain, the Shopify store or hosting account, analytics and any paid apps are set up in your name, and you get full admin access from day one."

**A warning sign:** the agency registers your domain or store in its own name, or says it will "transfer everything at the end". If the relationship goes wrong, you may have to fight to get your own business back.

## 2. What exactly is included in the price, and what is not?

A quote that says "Shopify store: £2,000" tells you very little. Ask for the scope in writing: how many page templates, how many products they will upload, which apps they will set up, how many rounds of design changes, and whether copywriting and product photos are included.

**A warning sign:** a low headline price with a vague scope. The difference usually comes back as "extras" halfway through the project.

## 3. What will this cost me every month after launch?

The build price is only part of the cost. Ask for a list of every running cost: platform plan, paid apps, premium theme renewals, hosting, email, and any monthly retainer the agency expects.

**A good answer** is specific and tells you which costs are optional. For a simple business website, a good agency may even tell you that you do not need a monthly platform at all.

## 4. Can I see work like mine, and can I click through it?

Screenshots are easy to fake and easy to polish. Ask for live links to sites similar to yours, in your industry or with the features you need, and open them on your own phone.

If the agency is new and shows concept or demo builds, that is fine, as long as they say so clearly. What should worry you is work presented as client projects that turns out not to be.

## 5. Who will actually do the work?

Some agencies sell the project and pass it to a freelancer or another studio. That is not always bad, but you should know. Ask who will design, who will build, and who you will speak to week to week.

**A good answer** names people and roles. You want one person who is responsible for your project from start to finish.

## 6. How will you make the site fast on mobile?

Most store traffic is on phones, and slow pages lose customers. Ask how they will keep the site fast: image handling, how many apps and scripts they add, and how they test speed.

**A good answer** mentions testing real pages, such as a product page, on mobile with a tool like Google PageSpeed Insights, and keeping apps to the ones you need. **A warning sign** is promising a "100 score" on a store full of apps.

## 7. What happens after launch?

Something always needs fixing in the first few weeks. Ask how long support lasts after launch, what it covers, how quickly they reply, and what ongoing help costs after that.

**A warning sign:** "We'll be around if you need anything" with nothing written down.

## 8. Will I be able to update the site myself?

You should not need a developer to change a price, add a product or update your opening hours. Ask what you will be able to edit yourself, and whether they will show you how.

**A good answer** includes a short handover: a walkthrough call or a recorded video of the tasks you will do most often.

## 9. How do you handle accessibility, privacy and the rules in my market?

If you sell to customers in the EU, the European Accessibility Act applies to many online stores. If you collect personal data in the UK or EU, GDPR applies, including cookie consent. In the US, inaccessible websites have led to lawsuits under the ADA.

You do not need an agency that is a law firm. You do need one that knows these rules exist and builds with them in mind, for example by working towards WCAG 2.1 AA for accessibility and setting up cookie consent properly. We have written a [guide to the European Accessibility Act for online stores](/blog/european-accessibility-act-online-stores) if you want the detail.

## 10. How do payments and timelines work?

Ask for the payment schedule (for example, a deposit and then payments at agreed milestones), the timeline with dates, and what happens if either side is late. Ask how changes to the scope are priced.

**A warning sign:** 100% payment upfront for a large project, or no timeline at all.

## The answers matter less than how they answer

A good agency will be happy to answer all 10 in writing before you pay anything. If questions about ownership, cost or who does the work make them uncomfortable, that tells you more than any portfolio.
