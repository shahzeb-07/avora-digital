If you sell online to customers in the European Union, your store now has a legal accessibility requirement. The European Accessibility Act (EAA) has applied since **28 June 2025**, and it covers e-commerce: the pages, product information and checkout that lead a customer to buy.

Many store owners outside the EU assume it does not apply to them. If you sell to consumers in the EU, it can, wherever your business is based. This guide covers what the law asks for in plain terms, and the checks that fix most of the common problems.

*This article is general information, not legal advice. Each EU country has its own law putting the EAA into effect, so check the rules for the countries you sell to, or ask a lawyer.*

## What the European Accessibility Act covers

The EAA is an EU directive that requires certain products and services to be usable by people with disabilities, including people who are blind or have low vision, are deaf or hard of hearing, or have limited movement or cognitive disabilities.

For online stores, the part that matters is **e-commerce services**: selling products or services to consumers online. That includes the journey to the sale, such as browsing, product pages, the cart, forms and checkout.

It applies to businesses that offer these services to consumers in the EU, **whether or not the business is in the EU**. A UK, US or Pakistani brand that ships to customers in Germany or France is in scope for those sales.

## Who is exempt

**Microenterprises** offering services are generally exempt from the service requirements. That means businesses with **fewer than 10 employees** and an **annual turnover or balance sheet of no more than €2 million**.

Two things to keep in mind:

- The exemption covers the service requirements. It does not make an inaccessible store a good idea. Customers with disabilities still cannot buy from it.
- Growing past either threshold brings you into scope. If you are close, it is cheaper to build accessibility in now than to fix a whole store later.

## What "accessible" means in practice

The EAA sets requirements in general terms. In practice, the reference most businesses work to is the European standard **EN 301 549**, which includes the **WCAG 2.1 Level A and AA** success criteria. WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) is the same standard most accessibility audits use worldwide.

You do not need to memorise WCAG. Most failures on online stores come from a small number of problems, and they are fixable.

## The 8 store checks that matter most

### 1. Every product image has meaningful alt text

Screen readers read alt text aloud. "IMG_4821.jpg" or an empty description tells a blind shopper nothing. Write what the image shows: "Navy linen shirt, front view, worn with sleeves rolled up". Purely decorative images should have empty alt text so they are skipped.

### 2. The whole store works with a keyboard

Try it yourself: put the mouse away and use only Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter and the arrow keys. Can you open the menu, choose a size, add to cart and reach checkout? Can you always see where you are on the page? Menus, pop-ups and size pickers built from custom code are where this usually breaks.

### 3. Text has enough contrast

Light grey text on white and white text on pale photos are common on stylish stores and hard to read for many people. WCAG AA asks for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. Free contrast checkers will tell you in seconds.

### 4. Forms have real labels and clear errors

Every field needs a visible label, not just placeholder text that disappears when you type. Errors should say what is wrong and how to fix it ("Enter a postcode, for example SW1A 1AA"), not just turn the field red.

### 5. Buttons and links say what they do

A screen reader user often jumps from link to link. Ten links all called "Shop now" or icon buttons with no name (a cart icon, a heart, a close "X") are confusing. Each one needs an accessible name that says what it does.

### 6. Pop-ups can be closed and do not trap people

Newsletter pop-ups and cookie banners must be reachable by keyboard, closable with the Escape key or a clearly labelled button, and must not trap focus behind them.

### 7. Video and audio have captions

Product videos with speech need captions. Autoplaying video should be avoidable or pausable, and nothing should flash rapidly.

### 8. The page works when zoomed

People with low vision zoom in. At 200% zoom, text should still fit, nothing important should overlap, and the store should not need sideways scrolling to read.

## Watch your apps and your theme

On Shopify and other platforms, many accessibility problems come from **apps and theme customisations**, not from the platform itself. Review widgets, upsell pop-ups, size pickers and custom sliders are common culprits.

A note on "accessibility overlay" widgets: a plugin that adds a toolbar to your site does not, by itself, make an inaccessible store accessible. Fix the underlying code first.

## The accessibility statement

The EAA also expects service providers to publish information about how their service meets the accessibility requirements, usually as an **accessibility statement** page. Keep it honest and practical:

- Which standard you work to (for example WCAG 2.1 AA)
- What is known not to work yet, and when you plan to fix it
- How a customer can contact you if they cannot complete an order

## What about the UK and the US?

- **UK:** the UK is not covered by the EAA itself, but UK businesses selling to EU consumers are covered for those sales. UK businesses also have duties under the Equality Act 2010 to make reasonable adjustments for disabled customers.
- **US:** there is no single federal technical rule for private online stores, but businesses have faced lawsuits under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) over inaccessible websites. WCAG is the standard these cases usually point to.

The practical answer is the same in all three markets: **work towards WCAG 2.1 AA**.

## Where to start

1. Run a free automated check, such as WAVE or Lighthouse, on your homepage, one product page, the cart and checkout.
2. Do the keyboard test from check 2 on the same pages.
3. Fix the issues on your **product page and checkout first**, because that is where a customer either buys or gives up.
4. Publish an honest accessibility statement and keep it updated.

Automated tools find only part of the problems. They are a starting point, not a pass mark.
